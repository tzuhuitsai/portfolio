Overview

As the sole UX designer on an 8-month SaaS initiative, I designed a data visualization editor from the ground up — balancing the needs of non-technical business users against the constraints of a complex frontend architecture. The core challenge was making advanced charting accessible without reducing its power.

My responsibilities spanned the full design process: competitive audits, sitemaps, wireflows, prototypes, weekly stakeholder presentations, and close collaboration with the engineering team through to implementation.

The Problem

Business users needed custom dashboards to monitor KPIs, but building them required engineering support — creating a bottleneck that slowed decision-making. Existing tools were either too limited for complex data or too technical for everyday users, leaving a gap the product needed to fill.

Goals

Enable non-technical users to build dashboards without engineering help

02

Research & Exploration

I began with a competitive audit of leading BI and data visualization tools — Tableau, Metabase, and Looker Studio — focusing on their editing experience, chart configuration flows, and how they handled beginner vs. power-user modes.

Key Finding

Most tools force users to choose between flexibility and simplicity — but the most successful products offered progressive disclosure, hiding complexity until users needed it.

From this insight, I designed the editor around a "simple first" principle: a minimal default state that exposes advanced configuration only when a user actively explores it. I then created sitemaps for developers to quickly understand the product structure, followed by wireflows to visualize layout and flow.

Key Design Decisions
Progressive sidebar — not a modal for chart config
Modals interrupt flow and force users to remember settings. A persistent sidebar lets users make changes while watching the chart update in real time, dramatically reducing trial-and-error.
Simplified chart picker with "recommended" defaults
Faced with 20+ chart types, most users froze. Surfacing 4–5 recommended types based on data shape reduced decision fatigue and sped up first-time chart creation.
Role-based view vs. edit modes at the layout level
Viewers and builders have fundamentally different mental models. Separating modes at the top level — rather than disabling individual elements — reduced confusion for both audiences and made permission design much cleaner for the engineering team.
Final Design
Hi-fi screens — covered under NDA

The final design shipped with a two-panel editor (canvas + config sidebar), a streamlined chart library organized by use case, and a role-based permission system that cleanly separated viewer and builder experiences. Clear Figma annotations were provided to the engineering team at each milestone.

Takeaways
①

Being the sole designer means you also own communication. Weekly stakeholder presentations taught me to frame design decisions in business language, not just UX rationale.

②

SaaS product design requires designing for a spectrum of users simultaneously. Progressive disclosure became my go-to pattern for resolving the simplicity vs. power tension.

③

Clear Figma annotations aren't optional — they're the contract between design and engineering. Investing extra time in specs saved multiples of that time in back-and-forth.
